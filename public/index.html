Part 01

Introducing the project

One-sentence summary
A network of people using AI to prototype more optimistic futures, run as an experiment lab — the network is the prototype, the experiments are its releases, the diary is the evidence.
Intended civic community
Civic tech, tech for good, open source, govtech, and grassroots groups — the communities most at risk of being priced out of AI — starting with the ones already engaged: Campaign Lab, the charity evaluators, the Newspeak House cohort and its publics.
Core user need
An AI upskilling gap, especially around agents, closing on someone else's terms — skills that need translating before the window shuts.
Core hypothesis
Keep a procedure's institutional skeleton and change only its intent, and the encounter transforms — designed affect on real procedure builds civic capability. And translation beats evangelism: meet a community inside its own work and the gap closes where a talk wouldn't reach.
Current version of the concept
A network for closing the AI gap for civic communities — the fun thing is the method, the upskilling window is the moment, govtech is the horizon. Flagship decision queued with faculty.

As AI continues to improve and permeate through everyday life, I find myself watching the gap between the people who have AI access and those who don't get wider — and that worries me. Growing up in the US as the child of immigrants, I've seen what happens to skilled workers from other countries: neuroscientists and engineers working at gas stations and driving taxis to get their children educated in America, because their degrees weren't legible to the system. I believe AI is going to make immigrants out of us all — that it's going to widen this gap, make it more and more expensive to access, to learn, and to teach.

Sparkle Bureaucracy started as a bet about bureaucracy: you can keep the entire procedural skeleton — the forms, the queues, the checkpoints, the stamps — and change only the intent, and the whole experience transforms. "Bureaucracy" carries the weight; it signals unexpected seriousness. "Sparkle" says it doesn't have to be miserable — it lowers threat, invites participation, and makes experimentation socially acceptable in environments that are usually defensive. The working ratio, on the record since the spring: 80% credible institution, 20% sparkle. Underneath it sits the long-term horizon: digital access to good civic services is load-bearing for democracy, and that's where the name comes from — running experiments that illustrate the power of technology and how emerging innovations can help us envision the possibilities in govtech.

I came into the fellowship thinking I wanted to run experiments on things like DMVs — what does a better licence renewal process look like? What does liquid democracy look like if the results are livestreamed while you can still change your vote? Then I ran an immigration scheme at my own birthday — passports, visa types, credentials, checkpoints — and watched what happened. People co-created the fiction; compliance felt like joining a story world; even rejection felt narrative rather than punitive. Ed Saperia looked at it and said: that's Sparkle Bureaucracy. The bet became the founding proof: same skeleton, different intent.

The experimentation widened from there, and each widening forced a decision. The cohort reunion became a spy game because nobody wanted to answer "how is your prototype going?" one more time — and proved that competition and games lower the bar to social interaction. When usable agent tooling suddenly arrived, I started Clawclub, because I'd been testing it in ways that excited me and wanted a community to be excited with — sometimes 10 people, sometimes 60 filling the hall. Leading the fellowship's awards process turned into Project Mirror and a fifteen-version evaluation pipeline published change by change — which is where the lab hit its first hard ethical line: watching a cohort-mate reflect live on what it feels like to have an agent built from your public record taught me that the inference is not consent, and that line now shapes everything the lab specifies about synthetic representation.

All these communities — the tech-for-good people, the emerging-technology builders, the RightsCon folks, the Protocols for Publishers summit, the vTaiwan delegation, the charity evaluators, the campaigners — helped me identify, collect, and narrow the characteristics of the community I want to build. And they surfaced the more urgent problem the original bet had to meet: AI is arriving like a new country arrives — suddenly skills need translating, credentials don't parse, and the people who can't afford the translation fall behind. The communities that should be shaping this technology — civic tech, tech for good, open source, govtech, the grassroots — are the ones most at risk of being priced out of it. The problem statement, as it now stands: the state is failing in many countries; AI offers an opportunity to fix this; we as technologists need to make sure people working at the grassroots are not left behind; and we can use AI to prototype and imagine a different future.

Getting there took a reckoning, and it's in the diary on purpose. In late July, advice on how networks are born wouldn't settle; the essay that worked out why found the founder's own loop — concealing the programme out of fear it would sound frivolous, which made the concept look vague, which earned advice to go lighter. Naming the loop broke it. An hour with Hannah O'Rourke then turned six months of thrash into a structure that resolved it in an afternoon: there isn't one thing here, there are three, and they don't need collapsing. The method — playful games, rituals, and working prototypes that let people feel how a system works instead of being told. The moment — civic and political groups have an AI upskilling gap, especially around agents, and it's closing on someone else's terms; while the window is open, SB tests the newest technology and builds the bridge to the people who need it. The horizon — govtech. It reduces to two questions anyone can ask: "What can I use AI for?" and "What can AI do for me?"

The lab also decided what counts, in writing. After a 100-case research sprint, the working definition:

Sparkle is the designed charge in a consequential procedure: rules and power become possible to feel and understand, people gain meaningful room to participate, question, or act, and the encounter leaves behind civic capability rather than a decorative veneer.

And the bar an experiment must clear: an institutional skeleton (a recognisable ritual — queue, permit, court, ledger, passport), an affective re-skin (participatory and legible without pretending power has disappeared), decision pressure (something is accepted, rejected, classified, delegated, or made contestable), and a durable artifact (a receipt, ledger, passport, constitution, or protocol that could harden into a reusable pattern). If an idea doesn't change who can act, how judgment is made, what becomes visible, or how a decision can be contested — it isn't strong enough yet.

So what is the prototype? The network is the prototype; the experiments are its releases; the diary is the evidence. The experiments and activations are the dated sequence in Parts 02 and 03. The working artifacts are live: Sparkle Border Authority, Project Mirror and the V1–V15 awards pipeline, Campaign Factory, Conference Pulse, Topic, and the site and mailing list at sparklebureaucracy.org. The open diary — weekly entries, experiment cards with risks and ethics notes, engagement and prior-art logs, the research corpus, and the essays where the project interrogated its own assumptions in public — is the complete record. Along the way the network started to demonstrate demand from inside the institution: faculty brought me work three times — Topic to build for Ed, Conference Pulse to a written brief from Hannah, and Campaign Factory against her provocation to make campaigners take agents seriously — commissions, dated in the commit history.

One honest caveat, stated up front: the name "Sparkle Bureaucracy" may not survive the network's next phase — making bureaucracy sparkle is my long-term horizon, but the urgent work is the gap. What's assessed here is the accumulated practice, the communities engaged, and the roadmap: a hackathon and demo night under the network's own banner, a flagship decision queued with faculty, and a rhythm determined with the community (Part 05).

Sparkle Border Authority: "Birthday Passports"

A full border-crossing ritual run live at my birthday — identity checks, purpose-of-visit declarations, secondary screening, visa classes with privileges, printed A6 visa stickers, checkpoints, admin overrides, and a live immigration stats dashboard (the app, still deployed). The language layer did real work: "sparkle compliance," "diplomatic glitter" — compliance felt like joining a story world, and even rejection flows felt narrative, not punitive. People co-created the fiction.

Hypothesis
keep the entire procedural skeleton of a border regime and change only the intent, and the experience transforms — without losing rigour.
Method
a real intake → process → decision → artifact → handoff pipeline, run live on guests, with printed artifacts and an admin back office. A birthday is the safest sandbox for studying how authority feels: temporary, consented power.
What happened
guests went through the full ritual and co-created the fiction; Ed Saperia saw it and said that's Sparkle Bureaucracy — the founding proof.
What I learned
same skeleton, different intent. And that even rejection can be designed to feel narrative rather than punitive.
Impact on next iteration
the four-part experiment bar (skeleton, re-skin, decision pressure, durable artifact) generalises this event; the missing piece — a post-event ritual — was added by the spy reunion.

30 Mar 2026experimentSB/EXP/03

Project Mirror + the V1–V15 awards pipeline

The capstone of leading the fellowship's open awards process: 321 political-technology projects evaluated through a pipeline whose every version (V1–V15) was published on GitHub with rationale for every change — from random scoring, through structured dossiers (Civic Tech Field Guide taxonomy, OpenAlex citations, ProPublica financials), to values-as-constitutions and multi-jury agent systems. The framing: rankings are political; openness makes the trade-offs discussable.

Project Mirror asked: what if all 18 cohort members had been in the room? Eighteen synthetic evaluator constitutions inferred from public records — criteria plus value modifiers (boosts for community ownership and under-resourced civic contexts; reductions for VC funding; a popularity discount to counter LLM familiarity bias) — then a Borda-count grand jury with strategic reasoning enabled. Winner: Liquid Feedback, stable under every manipulation attempt (18-point lead, 13 deliberation arguments, no revisions).

Hypothesis
publishing an evaluation algorithm version by version, with rationale, makes institutional judgment legible — and moves the argument from "trust us" to "argue with this specific choice".
Method
fifteen published algorithm versions; constitutions describing evaluative stance, never claimed beliefs; value modifiers making normative assumptions inspectable; the audience running its own live ranking (QR-code criteria → agent-generated ranking) before seeing the committee's.
What happened
the cohort was pulled in — Davit on values taxonomy, Gamithra's ITN/A framework, Alexandra on aggregation, Jamie on synthetic users, Fran building the People's Choice system with my support — and the showcase ran the full ritual live.
What I learned
transparency isn't trust, but it makes trade-offs discussable. And the tension that stays: watching Asil reflect live on what it feels like to have an agent built from your public record — the inference is not consent.
Impact on next iteration
Mirror Commons is specified in the idea bank as the answer — synthetic stand-ins people manage themselves, with editable evaluator passports, red lines, expiry, and revocation. From estimating people, to people governing their own estimates.

"A little bittersweet, because these are synthetic representations. But I think where we are is the cusp of something… technology that keeps us in the judgment loop and lets us work together better." — from the Project Mirror closing reflection

Technical
  • Core flow: research public profiles → inferred evaluator constitution per person → score 321 projects → ranked outputs with rationale → aggregation → Borda-count deliberation with strategic reasoning.
  • Key design choices: constitutions describe evaluative stance, not claimed beliefs; value modifiers make normative assumptions inspectable; a popularity discount counterbalances LLM familiarity bias; aggregation-method variation treated as evidence, not noise.
  • Every version published: 2025.newspeak.house/awards.
Evidence
  • The live awards site with the full V1–V15 history.
  • The showcase mechanic: the audience was handed the 321-project longlist and asked to rank it — and to note how they were deciding — before seeing the committee's method.

Campaign Factory

Built with Campaign Lab in an intense ten-day sprint — 122 commits, 62 of them on 15 July — against Hannah O'Rourke's brief: demo multi-agents, and make campaigners take agents seriously. Give Campaign Factory a UK local or public-policy problem and a place, and it researches the decision route, maps power and pressure, proposes strategy and tactics, and drafts campaign materials — showing the work as it happens, with Hannah's campaign-building knowledge base embedded in the system. Hypothesis

Method

translation beats evangelism, with a stated mechanism. Sometimes sparkle removes a ritual — the free-access code deleted a gate rather than decorating one. Conference Pulse

The live companion for the AI & Campaigning Conference, built in parallel to a written brief from Hannah O'Rourke: schedule, anonymous per-panel comments, votes, an organiser dashboard, a big-screen room view. Nine sessions, live all day.

Hypothesis
an instrument that samples participation repeatedly — rather than once at the door — can see what a room is actually doing, including when it disengages.
Method
built to the brief in the partner's organisation (CampaignLab/conference-pulse); anonymous per-panel voting and comments so participation costs nothing.
What happened
240 votes, 263 comments, and 28 private feedback messages across nine sessions — and the instrument saw the room: the comments between 14:00 and 16:00 match independent complaints about heat and breaks. Disengagement detected through participation data.
What I learned
repeat participation is measurable — and it's the hardest evidence a civic tool can produce. Also the year's most useful failure: 240 votes converted only 2 people to the mailing list on the day (the signup form was silently rejecting real people — found, fixed; the list stands at 15).
Impact on next iteration
the fifth condition — can an experiment detect its own defectors? — comes from this instrument, demonstrated rather than proposed. And the 30-day data purge it nearly lost its own evidence to became the lab's capture discipline.
Technical
  • Live schedule, anonymous per-panel comments and votes, organiser dashboard, big-screen room view; AI live-take summaries generated for 7 of 9 sessions.
  • The panel feedback was recovered from the production database ahead of a 30-day purge — no retention policy existed for the instrument's own evidence until then.
Evidence
  • From the production database (16 July, nine sessions): 240 votes · 263 comments · 28 private feedback messages · 67 unique voting devices (a floor on reach, not attendance — nobody who only watched the projector is counted).
  • Retention: 49 of 67 voters (73%) voted in two or more panels; 25 in five or more; three in all eight with voting open.

Part 05

The Roadmap

I'm still figuring out what this grows to be — and that's stated deliberately, not apologetically. What I know is what I care about, and the roadmap is built from it: there are three things here and they don't need collapsing. The method — playful, working experiments that let people feel how systems work — is what the network does. The moment — the AI upskilling window closing on civic communities on someone else's terms — is why it has to happen now. The horizon — govtech, digital access to good civic services as load-bearing for democracy — is where it's all pointed. Everything on this roadmap serves at least one of the three.

Why a network and not a product? Because the year's evidence says the network is what works: the experiments that landed, landed inside communities that had already settled they wanted to be in the room — the cohort, the Clawclub regulars, Campaign Lab, the charity evaluators. Instead of one tool hunting for users, the practice engages communities through experiments, and the communities keep pulling the work forward. The community formed around the practice before the network had a landing page — which, per the network-formation advice in the diary, is how networks are born.

The rhythm — what the network runs, and why

Each event type does a different job, learned from what worked this year:

  • Hackathon → demo night is the anchor cycle: build together around suggested civic topics, then show the work. It's Clawclub's lesson made regular — convene around the moment, and the building is the community.
  • Socials built as experiences (2× a year — game nights, Xmas, summer): the Birthday Passports and spy-reunion lesson. Games lower the bar to social interaction, so the experiments are the social infrastructure, not entertainment between the real work.
  • Hear-from-experts sessions — one domain at a time, when new things ship. The translation model from the keynotes: meet each community inside its own work.
  • Problem discovery — gatherings that bring in people with problems, not technologists: cross-pollination, coffee chats. The network's intake ritual, so the work starts from real civic needs.
  • Behind all of it, the mentor record's strategy: small, high-density events over big panels; a public calendar that curates the ecosystem; "easy yes" asks; a portfolio that includes other people's work — and an organiser to hold the rhythm.

Part 07

Final Reflection

What I set out to do

Find my mission — and test a bet: that you can keep bureaucracy's procedural skeleton, change only the intent, and transform the experience. I came in with DMV experiments and livestreamed liquid democracy in mind, expecting to spend the year making bureaucratic processes sparkle.

What I actually built

A network, run as an experiment lab. Nine deployed applications, a border regime at a birthday, an awards pipeline with every version published, a spy agency for a reunion, two artifacts commissioned by faculty and built with a partner community, a hack-night community that filled the hall, two keynotes that generated queues and follow-up demands, an open diary that became the data layer for this submission — and a specified idea bank of nine experiments waiting for the network's next phase.

What I learned about political technology

The year's learnings, in order of when they arrived:

  1. Same skeleton, different intent — you can change a system's intent without dismantling its structure, and the experience transforms.
  2. Games lower the bar — play reliably gets people into interactions they'd avoid as networking or compliance; the fun thing is the method, not decoration on it.
  3. Transparency isn't trust — but it moves the argument from "trust us" to "argue with this specific choice."
  4. The inference is not consent — synthetic representation scales participation and undermines it in the same motion; the lab's answer is people governing their own estimates.
  5. Translation beats evangelism — meet a community inside its own work, and the upskilling gap closes at small scale.
  6. Affect is broader than delight — relief, dignity, moral discomfort and productive disagreement all count; and the category's failure mode is affective legitimacy without recourse — a beautiful, friendly process that leaves power untouched. The thing SB must never build.
  7. The resistance is information — the hardest learning was about the founder: I trusted my execution far more than my right to originate authority, and naming that loop broke it. Legitimacy is constructed — borrow the answers of the people around you until your own catch up.
  8. The three registers don't need collapsing — six months of thrash came from trying to compress three cares into one mission; holding them as method, moment, and horizon resolved it in an afternoon.
  9. The lab's own bureaucracy is an experiment — and it ran: legible process, visible provenance, human judgment in the loop.

How the project changed

It started as the fun thing with a govtech horizon; it ends as a network for closing the AI gap for civic communities, with the fun thing as its method and the horizon intact. The encounters that changed it are dated in the Events Log, and the thinking that changed it is in Learning in Public — the communities I met narrowed who this is for and what it must never become.
