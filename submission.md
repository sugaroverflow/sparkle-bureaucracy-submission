## 01 | Introducing the project

As AI continues to improve and permeate through everyday life, I find myself watching the gap between the people who have AI access and those who don't get wider — and that worries me. Growing up in the US as the child of immigrants, I've seen what happens to skilled workers from other countries: neuroscientists and engineers working at gas stations and driving taxis to get their children educated in America, because their degrees weren't legible to the system. I believe AI is going to make immigrants out of us all — that it's going to widen this gap, make it more and more expensive to access, to learn, and to teach.

Sparkle Bureaucracy started as a bet about bureaucracy: you can keep the entire procedural skeleton — the forms, the queues, the checkpoints, the stamps — and change only the intent, and the whole experience transforms. "Bureaucracy" carries the weight; it signals unexpected seriousness. "Sparkle" says it doesn't have to be miserable — it lowers threat, invites participation, and makes experimentation socially acceptable in environments that are usually defensive. The working ratio, on the record since the spring: **80% credible institution, 20% sparkle**. Underneath it sits the long-term horizon: digital access to good civic services is load-bearing for democracy, and that's where the name comes from — running experiments that illustrate the power of technology and how emerging innovations can help us envision the possibilities in govtech.

I came into the fellowship thinking I wanted to run experiments on things like DMVs — what does a better licence renewal process look like? What does liquid democracy look like if the results are livestreamed while you can still change your vote? Then I ran an immigration scheme at my own birthday — passports, visa types, credentials, checkpoints — and watched what happened. People co-created the fiction; compliance felt like joining a story world; even rejection felt narrative rather than punitive. Ed Saperia looked at it and said: *that's Sparkle Bureaucracy.* The bet became the founding proof: same skeleton, different intent.

The experimentation widened from there, and each widening forced a decision. The cohort reunion became a spy game because nobody wanted to answer "how is your prototype going?" one more time — and proved that competition and games lower the bar to social interaction. When usable agent tooling suddenly arrived, I started Clawclub, because I'd been testing it in ways that excited me and wanted a community to be excited with — sometimes 10 people, sometimes 60 filling the hall. Leading the fellowship's awards process turned into Project Mirror and a fifteen-version evaluation pipeline published change by change — which is where the lab hit its first hard ethical line: watching a cohort-mate reflect live on what it feels like to have an agent built from your public record taught me that **the inference is not consent**, and that line now shapes everything the lab specifies about synthetic representation.

All these communities — the tech-for-good people, the emerging-technology builders, the RightsCon folks, the Protocols for Publishers summit, the vTaiwan delegation, the charity evaluators, the campaigners — helped me identify, collect, and narrow the characteristics of the community I want to build. And they surfaced the more urgent problem the original bet had to meet: AI is arriving like a new country arrives — suddenly skills need translating, credentials don't parse, and the people who can't afford the translation fall behind. The communities that should be shaping this technology — civic tech, tech for good, open source, govtech, the grassroots — are the ones most at risk of being priced out of it. The problem statement, as it now stands: the state is failing in many countries; AI offers an opportunity to fix this; we as technologists need to make sure people working at the grassroots are not left behind; and we can use AI to prototype and imagine a different future.

Getting there took a reckoning, and it's in the diary on purpose. In late July, advice on how networks are born wouldn't settle; the essay that worked out why found the founder's own loop — concealing the programme out of fear it would sound frivolous, which made the concept look vague, which earned advice to go lighter. Naming the loop broke it. An hour with Hannah O'Rourke then turned six months of thrash into a structure that resolved it in an afternoon: there isn't one thing here, there are three, and they don't need collapsing. **The method** — playful games, rituals, and working prototypes that let people *feel* how a system works instead of being told. **The moment** — civic and political groups have an AI upskilling gap, especially around agents, and it's closing on someone else's terms; while the window is open, SB tests the newest technology and builds the bridge to the people who need it. **The horizon** — govtech. It reduces to two questions anyone can ask: **"What can I use AI for?"** and **"What can AI do for me?"**

The lab also decided what counts, in writing. After a 100-case research sprint, the working definition:

> **Sparkle is the designed charge in a consequential procedure: rules and power become possible to feel and understand, people gain meaningful room to participate, question, or act, and the encounter leaves behind civic capability rather than a decorative veneer.**

And the bar an experiment must clear: an **institutional skeleton** (a recognisable ritual — queue, permit, court, ledger, passport), an **affective re-skin** (participatory and legible without pretending power has disappeared), **decision pressure** (something is accepted, rejected, classified, delegated, or made contestable), and a **durable artifact** (a receipt, ledger, passport, constitution, or protocol that could harden into a reusable pattern). If an idea doesn't change who can act, how judgment is made, what becomes visible, or how a decision can be contested — it isn't strong enough yet.

So what is the prototype? **The network is the prototype; the experiments are its releases; the diary is the evidence.** The experiments and activations are the dated sequence in Parts 02 and 03. The working artifacts are live: Sparkle Border Authority, Project Mirror and the V1–V15 awards pipeline, Campaign Factory, Conference Pulse, Topic, and the site and mailing list at [sparklebureaucracy.org](https://sparklebureaucracy.org). The open diary — weekly entries, experiment cards with risks and ethics notes, engagement and prior-art logs, the research corpus, and the essays where the project interrogated its own assumptions in public — is [the complete record](https://github.com/sugaroverflow/sparkle-bureaucracy-site/blob/main/submission/sparkle-bureaucracy.md). Along the way the network started to demonstrate demand from inside the institution: faculty brought me work three times — Topic to build for Ed, Conference Pulse to a written brief from Hannah, and Campaign Factory against her provocation to make campaigners take agents seriously — commissions, dated in the commit history.

One honest caveat, stated up front: the name "Sparkle Bureaucracy" may not survive the network's next phase — making bureaucracy sparkle is my long-term horizon, but the urgent work is the gap. What's assessed here is the accumulated practice, the communities engaged, and the roadmap: a hackathon and demo night under the network's own banner, a flagship decision queued with faculty, and a rhythm determined with the community (Part 04).

### 14 Dec – 1 Apr | experiment | The field-notes room

photos: 2

Before any of the events: a public-by-design Matrix room, `field-notes: fatima`, with an explicit contract that anything posted could become public. 294 messages over three and a half months — me thinking out loud, with a handful of others dropping in. The rabbit holes weren't all on-mission, but power and AI kept surfacing: the surveillance-pricing investigation, shadow governance and needing permission in rule-light systems, AI system observability, agentic workflow risk. Fifteen arXiv papers, a dozen GitHub repos, the Civic Tech Field Guide, gov.uk.

**Hypothesis:** thinking in public, under an explicit consent contract, produces a research corpus a project can actually build on — a diary that starts before it knows it's a diary.

**What happened:** when the room went quiet on 1 April, the export became the seed corpus: every fragment catalogued into the research inventory, and the raw export preserved in the open diary.

**Impact on next iteration:** the export seeded the research inventory, and the room's public-by-design contract became the diary's governance default.

### 11 Mar | experiment | Sparkle Border Authority: "Birthday Passports"

photos: 4

A full border-crossing ritual run live at my birthday — identity checks, purpose-of-visit declarations, secondary screening, visa classes with privileges, printed A6 visa stickers, checkpoints, admin overrides, and a live immigration stats dashboard ([the app](https://sparkle-border-authority.vercel.app/), still deployed). The language layer did real work: "sparkle compliance," "diplomatic glitter" — compliance felt like joining a story world, and even rejection flows felt narrative, not punitive. People co-created the fiction.

**Hypothesis:** keep the entire procedural skeleton of a border regime and change only the intent, and the experience transforms — without losing rigour.

**Method:** a real intake → process → decision → artifact → handoff pipeline, run live on guests, with printed artifacts and an admin back office. A birthday is the safest sandbox for studying how authority feels: temporary, consented power.

**What happened:** guests went through the full ritual and co-created the fiction; Ed Saperia saw it and said *that's Sparkle Bureaucracy* — the founding proof.

**What I learned:** same skeleton, different intent. And that even rejection can be designed to feel narrative rather than punitive.

**Impact on next iteration:** the four-part experiment bar (skeleton, re-skin, decision pressure, durable artifact) generalises this event; the missing piece — a post-event ritual — was added by the spy reunion.

> "That's Sparkle Bureaucracy." — Ed Saperia, on seeing Sparkle Border Authority

- Operational surfaces: applicant flow, manual visa creation, visitor signup, checkpoint assistance, admin overrides, reprints, live stats dashboard.
- Open-sourced at Anouk Ruhaak's request: [sugaroverflow/sparkle-border-authority](https://github.com/sugaroverflow/sparkle-border-authority).

### 30 Mar | experiment | Project Mirror + the V1–V15 awards pipeline

photos: 4

The capstone of leading the fellowship's open awards process: 321 political-technology projects evaluated through a pipeline whose every version (V1–V15) was published on GitHub with rationale for every change — from random scoring, through structured dossiers (Civic Tech Field Guide taxonomy, OpenAlex citations, ProPublica financials), to values-as-constitutions and multi-jury agent systems. The framing: rankings are political; openness makes the trade-offs discussable.

Project Mirror asked: what if all 18 cohort members had been in the room? Eighteen synthetic evaluator constitutions inferred from public records — criteria plus value modifiers (boosts for community ownership and under-resourced civic contexts; reductions for VC funding; a popularity discount to counter LLM familiarity bias) — then a Borda-count grand jury with strategic reasoning enabled. Winner: Liquid Feedback, stable under every manipulation attempt (18-point lead, 13 deliberation arguments, no revisions).

**Hypothesis:** publishing an evaluation algorithm version by version, with rationale, makes institutional judgment legible — and moves the argument from "trust us" to "argue with this specific choice".

**Method:** fifteen published algorithm versions; constitutions describing evaluative stance, never claimed beliefs; value modifiers making normative assumptions inspectable; the audience running its own live ranking (QR-code criteria → agent-generated ranking) before seeing the committee's.

**What happened:** the cohort was pulled in — Davit on values taxonomy, Gamithra's ITN/A framework, Alexandra on aggregation, Jamie on synthetic users, Fran building the People's Choice system with my support — and the showcase ran the full ritual live.

**What I learned:** transparency isn't trust, but it makes trade-offs discussable. And the tension that stays: watching Asil reflect live on what it feels like to have an agent built from your public record — the inference is not consent.

**Impact on next iteration:** Mirror Commons is specified in the idea bank as the answer — synthetic stand-ins people manage themselves, with editable evaluator passports, red lines, expiry, and revocation. From estimating people, to people governing their own estimates.

> "A little bittersweet, because these are synthetic representations. But I think where we are is the cusp of something… technology that keeps us in the judgment loop and lets us work together better." — from the Project Mirror closing reflection

#### Technical

- Core flow: research public profiles → inferred evaluator constitution per person → score 321 projects → ranked outputs with rationale → aggregation → Borda-count deliberation with strategic reasoning.
- Key design choices: constitutions describe evaluative stance, not claimed beliefs; value modifiers make normative assumptions inspectable; a popularity discount counterbalances LLM familiarity bias; aggregation-method variation treated as evidence, not noise.
- Every version published: [2025.newspeak.house/awards](https://2025.newspeak.house/awards).

#### Evidence

- The live awards site with the full V1–V15 history.
- Cohort contributions credited by name. (@todo the 18-agents-on-one-screen screenshot, a constitution excerpt)

#### Feedback

- "Please build some for the team." — a manager, on hearing about synthetic evaluator agents.
- Asil Sidahmed's live reflection at the showcase → the permanent ethical line, and the Mirror Commons spec.

### 2 Apr | experiment | The prototype diary is founded

photos: 2

The Matrix field-notes room went quiet on 1 April; the next morning the chat was exported and the diary repo was born the same day — which was also the day of the fellowship's **Prototype Previews**. The checkpoint arrived, and the diary became the deliverable: research inventory, sensemaking map, synthesis, and a README rewritten six times in one afternoon until it was in my voice.

**Hypothesis:** an agent-assisted diary — notes, commits, and logs turned into structured, human-approved entries — can keep an open record honest without consuming the founder.

**Method:** a strict human approval boundary, written down before the tooling: the agent may draft, classify, summarise, and suggest; it may not publish, contact collaborators, represent someone else's views, or mark feedback resolved without human approval.

**What happened:** the diary became the data layer for everything since — weekly entries, experiment cards, engagement and prior-art logs — and on 5 August the whole corpus merged into the public site repo, original repo archived. This submission is built directly on top of it, and is the experiment's first full run.

**What I learned:** the lab's own bureaucracy is an experiment — and it ran. Legible process, visible provenance, human judgment kept in the loop.

**Impact on next iteration:** the reconstructed entries are marked as such, gaps listed for human correction — the pattern every SB experiment now follows for provenance.

### 13 May | experiment | Cohort Reunion: Spy Edition (NHIFA)

photos: 4

Nobody in my cohort wanted to answer "how is your prototype going?" one more time — so the reunion became a spy agency. ### 16 Jul | experiment | Campaign Factory + Conference Pulse

photos: 5

Built with Campaign Lab in an intense ten-day sprint — 122 commits, 62 of them on 15 July — against Hannah O'Rourke's brief: demo multi-agents, and make campaigners take agents seriously. Give [Campaign Factory](https://campaign-factory.vercel.app) a UK local or public-policy problem and a place, and it researches the decision route, maps power and pressure, proposes strategy and tactics, and drafts campaign materials — *showing the work as it happens*, with Hannah's campaign-building knowledge base embedded in the system. Conference Pulse ran alongside as the live companion for the AI & Campaigning Conference at Newspeak House: schedule, anonymous per-panel comments, votes, an organiser dashboard, a big-screen room view.

**Hypothesis:** meeting a community inside its own work — their problems, their vocabulary, their knowledge base embedded in the system — beats evangelism; and designed affect in a consequential procedure can be *commissioned*, not just asserted.

**Method:** ten-day sprint in the partner's own GitHub organisation ([CampaignLab/campaign-factory](https://github.com/CampaignLab/campaign-factory), [CampaignLab/conference-pulse](https://github.com/CampaignLab/conference-pulse)); multi-agent decomposition so the work is showable and replayable — the architecture is the pedagogy; both artifacts run live at a nine-session conference.

**What happened:** the room's verdict was measured live by our own instrument — the end-of-panel vote on "Agents: working tools or over-hyped?" came back 19 ballots: 9 *could be useful with work*, 7 *already useful and real*, 3 *just hype*. The week after, a free-access code went onto the house key so "bring your own API key" stopped being the price of admission — a deliberate accessibility decision with a kill-switch as the safety valve. Campaign Lab sent Campaign Factory out in their newsletter.

**What I learned:** translation beats evangelism, with a stated mechanism. Sometimes sparkle *removes* a ritual — the free-access code deleted a gate rather than decorating one. And the sharpest question from the comment wall — how do we stop people's first experience being slop campaign plans? — is the quality bar Campaign Factory carries forward, not yet answered.

**Impact on next iteration:** "Campaign Factory, grown up" is a flagship candidate — already live, already partnered, already testimonialed — and the inbound pull it generated (Oxfam GB, the Civic Tech Field Guide) is shaping the autumn calendar.

> "Campaign Factory built for her what it would take her five months to research." — campaigner at the AI & Campaigning Conference (@todo get name/permission)

> "Incredible! would love to see a write up of how this was built." — from the Conference Pulse comment wall

#### Technical

- Campaign Factory: multi-agent pipeline that researches decision routes, maps power and pressure, proposes strategy and tactics, and drafts materials — with the run replayable end-to-end ([the conference replay](https://campaign-factory.vercel.app/factory/replay/conference)).
- Launched requiring the user's own Anthropic or OpenRouter key; free-access code on the house key the week after, with a kill-switch. Campaign Lab subsidises access.
- Conference Pulse: live schedule, anonymous per-panel comments and votes, organiser dashboard, big-screen room view; AI live-take summaries generated for 7 of 9 sessions.
- Both repos live in the partner's organisation — a trust signal, not a caveat.

#### Evidence

- **Retention: 49 of 67 voters (73%) voted in two or more panels**; 25 in five or more; three in all eight with voting open.
- The 19-ballot panel vote, preserved in full in the record.
- The instrument saw the room: comments between 14:00 and 16:00 match independent complaints about heat and breaks — disengagement detected through participation data.
- Inbound after the conference: Oxfam GB's campaign engagement manager requesting a team demo (September workshop being organised with Asil Sidahmed); the Civic Tech Field Guide inviting a show & tell demo; theory-of-change material offered for the strategy section.

#### Feedback

- The slop-campaign-plans question from the comment wall → logged as Campaign Factory's open quality bar.

## 06 | Final Reflection

**What I set out to do.** Find my mission — and test a bet: that you can keep bureaucracy's procedural skeleton, change only the intent, and transform the experience. I came in with DMV experiments and livestreamed liquid democracy in mind, expecting to spend the year making bureaucratic processes sparkle.

**What I actually built.** A network, run as an experiment lab. Nine deployed applications, a border regime at a birthday, an awards pipeline with every version published, a spy agency for a reunion, two artifacts commissioned by faculty and built with a partner community, a hack-night community that filled the hall, two keynotes that generated queues and follow-up demands, an open diary that became the data layer for this submission — and a specified idea bank of nine experiments waiting for the network's next phase.

**What I learned about political technology.** The year's learnings, compressed: **Same skeleton, different intent** — you can change a system's intent without dismantling its structure, and the experience transforms. **Games lower the bar** — play reliably gets people into interactions they'd avoid as networking or compliance; the fun thing is the method, not decoration on it. **Transparency isn't trust** — but it moves the argument from "trust us" to "argue with this specific choice." **The inference is not consent** — synthetic representation scales participation and undermines it in the same motion; the lab's answer is people governing their own estimates. **Translation beats evangelism** — meet a community inside its own work, and the upskilling gap closes at small scale. **Affect is broader than delight** — relief, dignity, moral discomfort and productive disagreement all count; and the category's failure mode is affective legitimacy without recourse — a beautiful, friendly process that leaves power untouched. The thing SB must never build. **The resistance is information** — the hardest learning was about the founder: I trusted my execution far more than my right to originate authority, and naming that loop broke it. Legitimacy is constructed — borrow the answers of the people around you until your own catch up. **The three registers don't need collapsing** — six months of thrash came from trying to compress three cares into one mission; holding them as method, moment, and horizon resolved it in an afternoon. **The lab's own bureaucracy is an experiment — and it ran.**

**How the project changed.** It started as the fun thing with a govtech horizon; it ends as a network for closing the AI gap for civic communities, with the fun thing as its method and the horizon intact. The communities I met through the year — vTaiwan's co-creation, the publishers' summit, the Bohm dialogue's lesson that expertise announced is expertise resisted, the Majority Myth launch pointing me at the model minority myth and my own motivations, Lumina House teaching me what being the bridge means — narrowed who this is for and what it must never become.
